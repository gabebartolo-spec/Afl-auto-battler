# Sir Doug Nicholls Round guernseys: design audit and cultural guardrails

ROADMAP ARD-M7-010 requires a club-by-club audit of every Sir Doug Nicholls / Indigenous Round
guernsey from 2014 before any implementation. This is the art agent's first pass (2026-10-07), at the
director's request: "do appropriate research on the art styles used or else it could become very ugly
very fast", and "leave scope for custom indigenous guernsey designs in the Forge".

- **Covered from official all-club roundups:** 2016, 2018, 2019, 2020, 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025 and
  2026 (afl.com.au), plus 2021 (National Indigenous Times). That is 180 club-seasons.
- **Gaps:** 2014, 2015 and 2017 (partial, see Part 5), and the AFLW, VFL/VFLW, SANFL, WAFL, NTFL and
  Tasmanian examples.
- **What's recorded:** only what the clubs and the AFL published. Stories and themes are
  paraphrased, never quoted at length. A dash means the source doesn't say.
- **No artwork is reproduced here.** The record exists so we know what was studied, and what must not
  be copied.

## Part 1: the law and the protocols (why this needs care beyond copyright)

- **Copyright protects each artist's work.** Every guernsey here belongs to its artist and club. We
  ship none of them without a licence, as the roadmap already says.
- **Style itself isn't protected by law yet, but it is protected by protocol, and law is coming.**
  - Creative Australia's *Protocols for using First Nations Cultural and Intellectual Property in the
    Arts* (Terri Janke) say the same things the AFL clubs do in practice:
    - First Nations people own their cultural heritage, including styles, symbols and stories.
    - Use needs consultation, consent, attribution and benefit-sharing.
    - Australian law doesn't yet stop the misuse of traditional symbols and stories.
  - The Productivity Commission's 2022 report on the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander visual arts
    and crafts market recommended new law to protect cultural expressions. It also recommended
    mandatory labelling of inauthentic products, meaning products in an Indigenous style that weren't
    made by, or licensed from, First Nations people.
  - The Commonwealth committed in *Revive* to stand-alone ICIP legislation covering traditional
    knowledge, cultural expressions and fake art. A First Nations expert working group and national
    consultations ran in 2024.
  - In January 2025 the government said it would develop that law. The proposals include disclosure for
    non-Indigenous products marketed in Indigenous styles. Its status in 2026 is still to be confirmed;
    no Act is known to have passed.
  - Sources:
    - https://creative.gov.au/investment-and-development/protocols-and-resources/protocols-for-using-first-nations-cultural-and-intellectual-property-in-the-arts/
    - https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries/completed/indigenous-arts/report
    - https://www.arts.gov.au/what-we-do/indigenous-arts-and-languages/protecting-indigenous-cultural-and-intellectual-property
    - https://nit.com.au/15-01-2025/15761/new-legislation-to-tackle-inauthentic-indigenous-art
- **What this means for us:**
  - Original "Indigenous-style" art made by the game is exactly the category those proposals target:
    a non-Indigenous product in an Indigenous style. The art agent is an AI, and nobody involved is a
    First Nations artist.
  - The roadmap's guardrails stop us copying works and inventing stories. But a design built from
    dot-infill, concentric "meeting places", U-shapes and animal tracks would still read as Aboriginal
    art made without Aboriginal people.
  - Attribution matters in practice. In 2021 Port Adelaide's guernsey art was first wrongly claimed by
    someone else, and the club went back to the real artist, Elle Campbell, for permission before
    using it (NIT 2021).

## Part 3: the design language worth learning (structure, not culture)

These are football-design moves: how clubs and artists rebuild a guernsey's own structure. None of them
belongs to a culture.
1. **The club's structure is the skeleton, transformed:**
   - stripes into story lanes (Collingwood 2023 and 2025, Hawthorn 2025, North 2024)
   - hoops into rivers or landscape bands (Bulldogs 2024 and 2026, Geelong 2025, where each hoop holds one sibling's panel)
   - the sash into a leaf, a landscape or a road (Richmond 2021 and 2026, Essendon 2016)
   - the "V" into wings, a river or a line of dots (Port 2023, 2024, 2025)
   - the club's emblem into a container (Fremantle's anchor 2021 and 2026, the GWS "G" 2024, the Fitzroy logo 2021)
2. **One focal shape at the heart of the front**, a quieter ground, and a few strong flowing lines that
   link them. The back carries a second, calmer motif or names.
3. **Horizontal zoning** (sky, land, water, top to bottom) reads well at small sizes, and hoops make it
   natural.
4. **Club colours stay in charge.** Most designs keep the club's base. Departures are deliberate and
   tied to the club: Fremantle purple, Hawthorn earth tones, Port's teal with an ochre-white.
5. **Colourway pairs:** the same art on a white or alternate base for clashes (Geelong 2026,
   Bulldogs 2024, St Kilda 2022 and 2024, GWS charcoal then white).
6. **Proportional ideas:** rings sized by counts, a number of points or dots standing for a number of
   players, names on the back.
7. **Asymmetry and wrap:** forms run diagonally, or wrap the torso like a cloak (West Coast 2019,
   Collingwood 2022).

## Part 4: what must never be used (culturally specific)

The roadmap's no-copy rules, made concrete from this audit:
- **Dreaming and creation beings:** the Waugul or Rainbow Serpent, Akurra, Bunjil, Waa, Jukurrpa.
- **Ceremony and ceremonial objects:** Kulama, pukumani poles, Milimika, Wangka, armbands,
  ceremonial body paint.
- **Torres Strait emblems:** the dhoeri/dhari headdress, which is on the Torres Strait Islander flag.
- **Clan- or region-owned techniques:** possum-skin cloak etchings (south-east), crosshatching or
  rarrk (Arnhem Land; south-west Victoria), Tiwi designs and colour sets, Western Desert iconography.
- **Specific people's material:** family totems, handprints and stencils, a player's pose or tattoos,
  language words and place names with cultural meaning.
- **The shared iconographic vocabulary itself:** dot-infill, concentric "meeting places", U-shapes for
  seated people, animal tracks, boomerangs and shields as motifs. This is what reads as "Aboriginal
  art", and it is what inauthentic products imitate. Using it without First Nations authorship is the
  harm the Part 1 protocols describe, even with no story attached.

## Part 6: why it gets ugly fast at our scale

Our footballers are pre-rendered sprites:
- The guernsey is a few dozen pixels across on a phone at 1x, a little over 100 at 3x.
- Its design is drawn by `figure.gdshader` from guernsey coordinates, with procedural primitives
  (stripes, hoops, sash, chevrons…).

The real guernseys are fine art printed at full scale. Shrunk to our scale:
- **Dot-work and crosshatching turn to noise.** They shimmer in motion and moiré on the stripes.
- **Thin lines alias.** The ideas that survive are the big ones: a recoloured hoop, a band, a wing
  "V", one focal shape.
- **Detail on the back is invisible** in side-on frames.
- **Too many colours muddy.** The club's two or three colours, plus at most one accent, read cleanly.

Any design, licensed or original, needs a **game adaptation**:
- at most 3-4 major shapes
- a minimum feature size of about 4 px at game scale
- strong value contrast
- the club colour hierarchy kept

For licensed art, the artist makes that simplification, or approves it.

## Part 7: options for the director

> **Decision (director, 2026-10-07): "you design it yourself, ask the boss to add import/export
> functionality so users can add their own designs."**
> - The art agent designs original Indigenous-round guernseys (option B), using Part 3's structural
>   moves only and nothing from Part 4. They carry no story, are labelled original in game, and hold a
>   cultural-review state in the catalogue.
> - The lead adds Forge import and export so players can add their own designs, in the format in
>   Part 8.
> - A style sample goes to the director before the full library (5-10 designs per club).

The roadmap already prefers commissioning Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander artists for the final
art. This audit adds one finding: the planned middle path (original designs in the learned style)
still makes the kind of non-Indigenous "Indigenous-style" product the protocols and coming law warn
against.

**Option A, recommended: First Nations artists make the art; we build everything else.**
1. Build the system now:
   - the per-club design catalogue with rotation, round scheduling, byes and saves (roadmap)
   - an **artwork layer** in the figure shader
   - the Forge's custom layer (Part 8)
2. Placeholders: club guernseys with a plain round trim, for example numbers or a collar band in the
   Aboriginal flag's colours. The flag has been free to reproduce since the Commonwealth acquired its
   copyright in 2022. No imitation art.
3. Commission or licence 5-10 designs per club from First Nations artists:
   - with a written ICIP agreement covering attribution, consent, payment and cultural review
   - each artist also approves the game-scale adaptation
   - this is how every real guernsey in Part 2 was made
4. The director chooses the people and pays; the art agent can't contract anyone.

**Option B: original designs in the learned style, as the roadmap now reads.**
- They would use Part 3's structural moves only, with none of Part 4, labelled in game as original,
  with cultural review before release.
- **Risk:** without First Nations authorship they still read as "Aboriginal art", and may be caught by
  coming labelling or prohibition rules.

**Option C: no Indigenous-style art at all.**
- The round is honoured through presentation: the name, Sir Doug Nicholls' story, flag-colour trims and
  numbers, the clubs' round names (Kuwarna, Walyalup, Narrm, Yartapuulti, Euro-Yroke, Waalitj Marawar).
- Licensed designs are added if and when they're commissioned.

## Part 8: room in the Forge

- **An artwork layer on the guernsey:** the figure shader samples a design texture in the existing
  guernsey coordinates, the ones already used for numbers.
  - The same layer serves the game's Indigenous-round library and the player's own designs.
  - **The agreed design format** (2026-10-07), shared by the art agent's library and the lead's Forge
    import/export:
    - A 256×128 PNG: the left half is the front, the right half the back.
    - x runs across from the wearer's right (0) to their left (1); y runs up from the hem (bottom
      row) to the collar.
    - R = pattern-colour weight, G = second-pattern-colour weight; the base colour shows elsewhere.
      These are the procedural designs' channels, so a design takes its club's three colours.
    - Export adds a small JSON: name, author, version.
    - An imported full-colour image is quantised to the three club colours, with a preview.
- **The player's own design:**
  - Paint in the Forge with simple neutral tools: lines, fills, shapes, the club's colours.
  - Or import an image the player made, rights confirmed by them.
  - Saved with the club, shown in a live preview, and simplified to game scale by the same rules as
    Part 6.
- **No ready-made "Indigenous" stamp kits.** No dot brushes, U-shapes or track stamps offered as an
  "Indigenous style". That would be the game manufacturing the style. A player may draw what they like;
  the game doesn't hand them someone's culture as a preset.
- **The data shape is already in the roadmap:** design id, club, variant, colour mapping, primitives or
  texture, provenance, real-vs-original flag, artist attribution, licence state, cultural-review state.
  Player designs add an owner field and a "made by the player" flag.
